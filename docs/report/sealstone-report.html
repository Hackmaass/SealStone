1  Summary

Milestone payments are common in freelance work, construction and grant funding, and they go wrong in three predictable ways. The agreement is written in prose that two people read differently. The file that gets approved is not the file that was reviewed. Or the money sits with an intermediary whose rules nobody can inspect. Sealstone is an escrow built to close each of these gaps, and to let anyone test that it has.

We organised the product around three seals.

SealWhat it guaranteesHow it is enforced
TermsThe agreement cannot be funded until every unclear term has been settled by a person, and neither side can later claim different words.The confirmed checklist is reduced to canonical JSON and hashed with keccak256. Both the client and the provider call sign(hash) on the contract before any money moves.
WorkThe client approves one specific deliverable. A different file cannot be approved in its place.The provider records the SHA-256 of the deliverable when marking a milestone complete. approve takes a hash and reverts unless it matches the stored one.
MoneyFunds move only through defined transitions, only for the party allowed to trigger them, and the books always balance.A role-gated state machine with pull payments, a reentrancy guard and a live reconciliation view, backed by 138 contract tests and an Attack Lab that fires real adversarial transactions.

Artificial intelligence has a narrow, deliberate job. It reads the agreement text and proposes a checklist, flagging terms that look ambiguous. It does not decide anything about money. A deterministic validator re-checks every figure and party, a person must resolve each flagged item, and only the confirmed result can be written to the chain. The booklet asks for exactly this boundary, and we kept it visible in the code as a type distinction between a proposed and a confirmed checklist.

The prototype runs entirely on a local Hardhat chain with a rules-based fallback, so the demonstration works without a network connection. Where this report cites test figures they come from the project's progress log, and where something has not been built yet we say so. Sections 10 and 11 list what remains open.

2  The problem we were given

The VORTEXA 3.0 booklet describes problem B-03 as follows. We checked the text below against the booklet PDF on 11 October 2026.

The Challenge. Build a milestone-based escrow in which simulated funds are released or refunded only when the agreement's conditions are met. The agreement names a client, a provider, and an arbiter who resolves disputes, and defines each milestone's amount and approval conditions.

The booklet then lists five requirements, which we paraphrase here.

  1. Implement the logic as a Solidity contract tested on a local Hardhat network. No real cryptocurrency and no public deployment are required.
  2. Enforce the lifecycle. The provider marks a milestone complete, the client approves it or disputes it, and the arbiter releases or refunds disputed funds. Each step belongs to one party and undefined transitions are rejected.
  3. Prevent duplicate releases, unauthorised refunds and out-of-order transitions, and keep the contract balance reconciled with deposits and payments.
  4. Back each guarantee with automated tests, including a second release attempt, an unauthorised refund, an out-of-order transition and a re-entrancy attack.
  5. AI may turn agreement text into a proposed checklist with ambiguities. A person must confirm it, ambiguities must be resolved, and AI output must never be written to the contract directly.

The booklet's common rules for every team also apply. The deliverable must be a working MVP that judges can test live. AI must be used meaningfully, which excludes a chatbot or a call that only rewrites text. Where a decision has real consequences, the outcome must follow explicit, checkable rules, and AI "must not silently override them". Data must be fictional, with no real personal records, real financial transactions or live third-party systems. External AI may be used, but "the core demonstration must not depend on them; provide a local fallback."

Where we chose to be stricter than the booklet

Two of our working rules go further than the text. We require the full demo to run with the network switched off, whereas the booklet asks only for a local fallback. We also avoid public networks by default, whereas the booklet says a public deployment is not required. We treat both as our own standards. The booklet publishes no judging rubric, so we have not assumed any scoring weights.

Why this is difficult

Code that holds money has no undo. A payment released twice cannot be recovered, a missing access check lets the wrong person take funds, and a re-entrant call can drain a balance before the contract updates its own records. The text side is just as unforgiving, because a sentence such as "the remaining funds after the first two milestones" leaves the amount undefined at the moment it matters. A good solution has to handle both halves, and the two meet at the point where prose becomes numbers on a ledger.

3  Proposed solution

3.1  Three layers, one flow

Sealstone treats the agreement, the deliverable and the funds as three separate things that can each be tampered with, and protects each one separately.

Agreement integrity. The extractor proposes a checklist and a list of ambiguities. A shared validator then checks parties, arithmetic and wording on its own, and can add or escalate flags but never remove one. A person resolves every flag. The confirmed checklist is serialised as canonical JSON, hashed, and stored in the contract as an immutable value. Neither party can fund until both have signed that same hash.

Deliverable integrity. When the provider marks a milestone complete they submit a SHA-256 hash of the deliverable. To release the money the client must present that exact hash. If a different file has been swapped in, the call reverts with DeliverableHashMismatch and shows both hashes.

Fund integrity. Payments are credited to a balance and withdrawn by the recipient (a pull payment). The state machine rejects every undefined transition with a named error. A reconciliation view compares the contract's real balance with what its own records say it should hold, and reports any ether that was force-sent from outside as surplus that can never be spent.

3.2  The demonstration scenario

The built-in scenario is fictional. A campus incubation cell funds a student team, KrishiLens, which is building a crop-disease photo app, with a 5 ETH grant across three milestones. A faculty mentor acts as arbiter and is fixed when the contract is deployed. The figures below are taken from the seeded checklist, samples/krishilens.confirmed.json.

No.MilestoneAmountDeadlineDeliverable
M1Problem validation report1.0 ETH31 Oct 2026Document

The source agreement text was written to contain real ambiguities: a payment tied to "the mentor's satisfaction", an amount described only as the remaining funds, a bonus clause that makes the total unclear, and a final milestone with no date. The rules extractor raises five flags on this text. A live language model run raised more, as section 7 describes.

3.3  User flow

  1. Paste or load an agreement. The service proposes a checklist and lists ambiguities, each with the sentence that caused it.
  2. Resolve every ambiguity. Blocking items must be edited or the clause removed. A warning can be acknowledged. The Confirm button stays locked until the gate is satisfied.
  3. Confirm. The checklist is hashed and the contract is deployed with the parties, the milestone amounts and the agreement hash.
  4. The client and then the provider each call sign(hash). These are ordinary on-chain transactions, not off-chain signatures.
  5. The client funds the exact total.
  6. The provider submits milestone one with the SHA-256 of the file. The client approves with that hash, or disputes with a reason. If the client does not respond within the review window the provider may escalate.
  7. For a disputed milestone the arbiter releases or refunds. Released money is credited to the provider and refunded money to the client, and each withdraws their own credit.
  8. When the last milestone reaches a final state the agreement closes. At any point anyone can open the vault page, see the ledger reconcile and re-hash the stored terms against the chain.

3.4  Interface

The web application has three working areas. The agreement wizard handles steps one to five. The vault page shows each milestone with the actions the current role may take, an activity timeline built from contract events, a ledger panel, and a Verify panel that re-hashes the terms in the browser and compares the result with the chain. The Attack Lab, described in section 8, lets a visitor run adversarial transactions and see the contract's response. Roles are switched with Hardhat's unlocked test accounts, so there is no wallet extension in this prototype.

4.2  Why the boundary is a type, not a convention

The checklist exists in two forms in the code. A ProposedChecklist is what the extractor returns and may contain anything. A ConfirmedChecklist exists only after the gate has accepted it. The function that deploys a vault takes a confirmed checklist, recomputes its hash, and reads the stored hash and total back from the chain after deployment. A small file, deploy.boundary.ts, contains a line that must fail to compile: it tries to pass a proposed checklist to the deploy function. If someone ever loosens the types so that line compiles, the type-check step fails and tells them.

4.3  The agreement hash

The hash is keccak256 of the UTF-8 bytes of the confirmed checklist in canonical form. Canonical JSON sorts object keys recursively using the default UTF-16 order, uses no whitespace, keeps arrays in order, and writes amounts as decimal wei strings. The serialiser throws on values that have no single canonical form, such as undefined, big integers, NaN and non-plain objects. There is exactly one implementation, in shared/src/canonical.ts, and a frozen test vector with deliberately unsorted keys and non-ASCII text. We cross-checked that vector's canonical string against an independent Python implementation. The confirmed JSON is stored by the server under its hash, so anyone can re-hash it later using the Verify panel.

5  Contract design

One MilestoneVault deployment is one agreement. The client, provider and arbiter are immutable, non-zero and pairwise distinct. The agreement hash, review window and total amount are immutable too. An agreement holds between one and twenty milestones. The specification in docs/04-CONTRACT-SPEC.md is the source of truth for everything in this section.

Other functions are sign (client or provider, once each, with the right hash), fund (client, exact total, payable), withdraw (anyone with a credit, non-reentrant), and the read-only reconcile, milestoneCount and getMilestone. Both receive and fallback revert with DirectPaymentNotAccepted.

Checks inside every function run in a fixed order: role, then agreement state, then index, then milestone state, then any extra checks. We made that order part of the specification so the Attack Lab always reports the first rule that failed, and so a reviewer reading a revert knows which layer refused.

5.3  Events and errors

Every state change emits an event, and the web timeline is built from them: AgreementSigned, AgreementFunded, MilestoneCompleted, MilestoneApproved, MilestoneDisputed, MilestoneEscalated, DisputeResolved, Credited, Withdrawn and AgreementClosed. There are no revert strings. Failures use custom errors that carry the values needed to explain them, for example InvalidMilestoneState(index, actual), MilestoneOutOfOrder(index, expectedIndex), DeliverableHashMismatch(expected, provided) and ReviewWindowOpen(escalationAllowedAt).

5.4  Reconciliation

The reconcile() view returns what was deposited, released, refunded and withdrawn, what is held in escrow and by open milestones, what is owed to the parties, the balance the books expect, the balance the contract actually holds, and any surplus. Its ok flag is true when the real balance is at least the expected balance, escrow equals the sum of open milestones, and what is owed equals released plus refunded minus withdrawn. Every subtraction in it is guarded so that the view itself never reverts, even if the balance were somehow lower than expected, in which case it reports ok = false.

5.5  Invariants

These are asserted after every step of the randomised test described in section 8.

I1The real balance is at least the expected balance, and equal unless ether was force-sent, in which case the difference is reported as surplus.
I2The deposited total is either zero or exactly the agreed total.
I3Funds held in escrow equal the sum of milestones that are still pending, completed or disputed.
I4The released and refunded totals equal the sums of the milestones in those states.
I5Amounts owed to the parties equal released plus refunded minus withdrawn.
I6A final milestone never changes state, and a failed call changes no storage.
I7Every milestone beyond the next one is still pending.
I8The arbiter's credit is always zero. The arbiter can never be paid.

The contract uses no tx.origin, no delegatecall and no selfdestruct. The one selfdestruct in the repository is in ForceSender, a test-only contract that exists to prove the surplus accounting, and the compiler warns about it. 7  The AI component

7.1  What AI does, and what it does not

The model turns agreement prose into a proposed checklist of parties, milestones, amounts, conditions and deadlines. It also classifies ambiguities by type and quotes the sentence that caused each one. It does not write to the contract, decide whether money moves, or recommend a ruling. The pipeline is: the model proposes, the validator checks, a person resolves and confirms, the result is hashed, both parties sign, and then the vault is deployed and funded.

7.2  Providers and fallback

The service picks a provider in this order: a local Ollama model if it answers within 1.5 seconds and the configured model is installed, then Gemini if an API key and model name are configured, then the built-in rules extractor, which is always available and deterministic. Model output is parsed with zod; on a parse failure the service retries once with the error appended, and falls back to rules if that also fails. Every response reports which source produced it, the model, the latency and the reason for any fallback. If the service itself is unreachable, the web app runs the same rules in the browser so the wizard still works, and says so on screen.

After parsing, the rules overwrite some of what the model said. The ambiguity identifier, its severity, its origin and each condition's "objective" flag are set by the rules, not the model, and the milestone index is simply the position in the list. A quote the model gives that cannot be found in the source text, allowing for case, spacing and curly quotes, is replaced with nothing and noted.

7.4  Validator and gate

The validator in shared/ runs on every proposal, whatever produced it, and the server and the browser run identical code. It checks that all three parties are present and distinct, that there are between one and twenty milestones, that every amount is present and positive, that the milestone amounts sum exactly to the stated total (compared in wei), that each milestone has at least one objective condition, and that the wording does not contain vague, conditional or automatic-transition phrases. It also checks grounding: every non-null amount must appear as "number ETH" in the source text, and every quote must be a substring of it. The validator may add or escalate a flag, but it never removes one the model raised.

The Confirm button is enabled only when four conditions hold. Every ambiguity has a resolution. Every blocking ambiguity was resolved by editing or removing the clause, since acknowledging it is not enough. Re-running the validator on the edited checklist finds no blocking issue, because an edit can introduce a new one. And each of the three roles has a distinct address. The server recomputes what must be resolved from the original proposal and source text, so a caller that submits an empty ambiguity list cannot skip the first two conditions, and a test covers that case.

7.5  What we have measured so far

We ran a real local model, qwen2.5:7b-instruct, on the KrishiLens text. It extracted the parties, total, titles, amounts and deadlines correctly. A cold first call took 26.8 seconds against a 30 second timeout, a warm call took 19.6 seconds, and the wizard run recorded 25.3 seconds. The model also raised two flags beyond the expected five. One was a reasonable vague-condition warning. The other, UNDEFINED_TRANSITION on the third milestone, was a false positive, because nothing in the text releases money automatically. A person had to clear it by removing the clause. We kept that behaviour on purpose: a false alarm costs one click, whereas a missed ambiguity can cost the agreement.

Not yet available. The planned evaluation (prompt P06), which would report precision, recall and false-positive rate for the rules extractor against the language model on labelled agreements, has not been run. It depends on the teammate's set of sample agreements and expected labels, which are not yet written. This report therefore quotes no accuracy figures for the AI component, only the single recorded run above.

8.1  The four required tests

The booklet's four adversarial tests exist under these exact names in contracts/test/Adversarial.test.ts: rejects a second release attempt, rejects an unauthorized refund, rejects an out-of-order transition and blocks a re-entrancy attack. The same file also holds further named guarantees, such as rejecting a wrong funding amount, early escalation (including one second before the boundary), and withdrawing twice.

8.2  The Attack Lab

The Attack Lab turns the adversarial tests into something a judge can operate. It offers twelve attacks and one contrast, each a real transaction sent from sandbox accounts. Every attack deploys its own fresh three-milestone vault, so no result depends on a previous one and the demo vaults are never touched. An end-to-end check compares the seeded vault's events, state, balance and code before and after 39 sandbox runs to confirm this.

The lab reports one of three outcomes: a pass (the contract did what was expected), FAILED (the contract did something else), or a lab error (the lab could not finish). Only a pass counts as blocked, and the score is computed from the results. The contrast row runs the same re-entrancy pattern against the deliberately vulnerable NaiveEscrow, which loses 3 ETH, to show that the attacker is real and that the real contract's refusal is meaningful. Three consecutive "Run all" executions without a page reload each blocked twelve of twelve with the ledger reconciled after every attack.

11  Limitations and known issues

We would rather state these plainly than have a reviewer find them.

  • It is a prototype on a local test chain. Roles are switched using Hardhat's unlocked accounts, there is no wallet extension, and nothing has been deployed to a public network. The project has had no external security audit.
  • The AI is only partly measured. There is one recorded live run and no accuracy statistics. With a live model, false positives such as the one described in section 7.5 must be cleared by a person.
  • Model latency is close to its timeout. A cold local model took 26.8 seconds against a 30 second limit and could fall back to rules. A warm-up call at server start is planned.
  • Long agreements are handled unevenly. The extract call grounds and validates against the first 6,000 characters, while the gate and the source hash use the whole text. The web interface therefore drives its cards from the gate check, not from the extract response.
  • An extreme review window can panic. The escalation time is computed as completedAt + reviewWindow in checked 64-bit arithmetic, so a value near 264 would revert with a panic instead of a custom error. A cap in the constructor would fix it; the specification has not been changed.
  • Gemini has not been tested against the real service, because no key was available. It is covered only by tests against a fake server.
  • Test-only code. ForceSender uses selfdestruct and the compiler warns about it. It is not part of the product.
  • Tooling notes. The production web bundle is a single 646 kB chunk, and the dependency install reports audit warnings in transitive development packages of the Hardhat toolchain. On the Windows development machine, local loopback connections were intermittently refused, which occasionally made an end-to-end run fail until it was repeated.
  • A testnet configuration exists but is not used. A network entry for the MST testnet is registered only when a deployer key is present in a local environment file. No deployment has been made, and the web app is not set up to use it.

13  Related work

We looked at escrow and attestation projects from hackathons and grant programmes. We separate confirmed winners from projects whose placement we could not verify, and we only call something a winner when a source says so.

ProjectStandingApproach, and how Sealstone differs
Agent Escrow1st place, APU Web3 AI hackathonEvery stage enforced on-chain, with a reentrancy guard. Pays automatically after a review period. Sealstone escalates to the arbiter instead, because an automatic payment is an undefined transition.
Chainlink Convergence 2026 winnersHackathon winnersCheck actions before they execute. We take the framing as a gate in front of money, applied to agreement text and contract calls.
Vernari Protocol, LinkPalWinners, earlier Chainlink hackathonsEscrow released on verified outside data, through oracles. Sealstone releases against a hash that the client checks, which keeps it working offline.
Attestify, AivyWinners, Hedera ApexAttestations and agent escrow. We note them as influences on the seal receipt idea.
Trustless Work, BoundlessGrants from the Stellar Community FundRole-separated and milestone-based escrow. These are funding awards, not hackathon wins. We borrowed the role model and the "submit proof" step.
TaskLoop, WorkProof, ArbitrAI, TrustArc AI Escrow, AI-Trustless EscrowPlacement not confirmedThese let AI review work or rule on disputes, sometimes with a confidence threshold or a vote of several models. Sealstone does not allow that, since the booklet forbids AI output deciding or being written to the contract.

The combination we believe is distinctive is the pairing of an ambiguity gate that blocks funding until a person has resolved every flagged term, a client approval bound to an exact deliverable hash, an Attack Lab that visitors can operate against a deliberately vulnerable contrast, and a live reconciliation that surfaces force-sent ether. We did not find that combination in the projects we reviewed, but we reviewed only a limited number and cannot claim it is unique.
